The Gromov–Wasserstein problem
Reference page / the optimization problem

Match relationships when features cannot be compared directly. Suppose two datasets describe
diﬀerent collections of objects, or two diﬀerent measurements of the same objects. Their feature spaces
may be incompatible. Gromov–Wasserstein (GW) matching uses the distances within each dataset: a
good correspondence pairs objects so that their relationships are preserved. It does not require a distance
between an object in one feature space and an object in the other.
Data and unknown. Let A ∈ Rn×n and B ∈ Rm×m be the two symmetric distance matrices. Positive
probability vectors a and b specify the mass of each object; uniform weights give ai = 1/n and bj = 1/m.
The unknown is a soft matching P ∈ Rn×m : Pij is the mass assigned between source object i and target
object j. Balanced matching requires

                                    U (a, b) = {P ≥ 0 : P 1m = a, P ⊤ 1n = b}.

The row and column sums prescribe how much each object contributes. They do not force a one-to-one
matching.

Two simultaneous matches, i ↔ j and k ↔ ℓ, incur a cost when Aik and Bjℓ disagree. Equivalently,
f (P ) is the expected squared distance discrepancy from two independent draws from P . This convention
includes neither a factor 1/2 nor entropy regularization. The objective is smooth and generally nonconvex;
the feasible transport polytope is convex. A small loss alone does not certify a global optimum or a
scientiﬁcally correct correspondence.

FraMES combines a momentum estimate of ∇f (P ) with this current penalty gradient. Its linear mini-
mization oracle places each row’s mass in a minimizing column. Convex-combination updates preserve
nonnegativity and row masses; column masses can be violated during optimization.
What a results page measures. Random probes estimate the gradient of the same ﬁxed GW objective;
they do not subsample the dataset or impose a rank restriction on P . Reported feasible loss is f evaluated
after repairing an output to satisfy both marginals to numerical tolerance. The repair is a diagnostic, not
an optimization step. Raw loss, feasibility error, and any smoothed penalty are distinct quantities. Loss
magnitudes depend on distance scaling, so reductions are interpreted within a ﬁxed dataset and objective
convention. Timing deﬁnitions and the reported output statistic belong on the experiment page.

BMMC data: increasing the probe count
Experiment results / veriﬁed 9 October 2026                                                                                     9 of 9 runs completed

Data. Bone-marrow mononuclear cells (BMMC): all 6,224 cells in each of the downloaded processed
moscot RNA and ATAC ﬁles, with no additional cell subsampling (38,738,176 coupling variables). RNA
uses 50 provided PCA coordinates; ATAC uses 38 provided LSI coordinates, normalized rowwise to unit
Euclidean norm. Within-view Euclidean distances are divided by each matrix’s own maximum. The views
are independently reordered; masses are uniform and labels are unused.

Markers are saved checkpoints; lines are visual guides. Shading shows the three-seed IQR. The time plot places

Assessment. Increasing the probe count improves the ﬁnal feasible loss at 20,000 updates, but the
decrease remains modest and solver cost rises. The 2,048-probe median reduction is 1.471%, versus
0.268% with 128 probes. These are three random seeds on one ﬁxed processed dataset, not independent
biological replicates. The result does not establish that gradient noise is the only remaining limitation;
biological matching accuracy was not evaluated.
Scope. This comparison changes only the probe count within the stated conﬁguration. It is not a
comparison against another optimization method, nor a claim that these prefactors are the best ones for
BMMC. The processed ﬁles used here are not the entire larger original BMMC collection.


Measurement. Final-iterate loss after transport repair, evaluated in FP64; initial and intermediate losses use
FP32. This is neither a best-checkpoint loss nor a theorem-selected output. Solver time includes initialization and
updates; it excludes preparation, repairs, loss diagnostics, logging, snapshots and ﬁnal FP64 validation. It is not
end-to-end elapsed time.
